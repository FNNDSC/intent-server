= Reasoning, LLMs, and Agentic Programs: Agents Will Always Lie, So Clinical Truth Must Live Outside the Model
Author: Rudolph Pienaar
:doctype: article
:stem: latexmath

== Abstract

Modern AI is not magic; it is mathematics. This paper uses that lens to formalize a key insight, make a theorem-level claim about orchestration risk, and derive an architectural consequence. Modern AI systems rest on neural networks that, in their classical predictive role, are deterministic: for fixed parameters and an input, the forward pass produces the same output. These networks often act as compressive maps from high-dimensional inputs onto lower-dimensional internal representations. Generative AI uses the same deterministic machinery internally, but for a different task: given a context that underdetermines many admissible continuations, the model produces a distribution over possible outputs and a decoding rule selects one continuation. Probability is therefore not an optional detail layered on top of an otherwise deterministic mechanism; it is intrinsic to the generative step. The fallacy is to place this probabilistic generator in charge of workflow assembly, tool selection, and multi-step orchestration, including in clinical settings, without appreciating the mathematical limits that follow. The very machinery that enables generation also makes non-trivial, safety-critical workflows structurally unsafe when generated outputs are allowed to determine execution over a large space of possible procedures.

Theorem (non-zero orchestration risk): once a probabilistic policy is responsible for exploring a large space of possible action sequences that contains both valid and invalid workflows, the probability of selecting an invalid workflow cannot be driven to zero without eliminating runtime freedom in workflow assembly. I make this precise by modeling tools as nodes in a directed graph, workflows as paths, and an agent as a probability distribution over those paths. Under mild assumptions, any non-trivial agentic policy over this graph has non-zero mass on clinically invalid paths; as workflows grow longer and tools more numerous, the probability of at least one unacceptable orchestration choice tends to increase rather than decrease. In this sense, such systems will always “lie” in the sense that matters in clinical contexts: they will inevitably produce procedurally incorrect or unsafe workflows with non-zero probability. Over roughly the last year, however, vendors and research groups have begun to promote increasingly agentic systems for healthcare and other high-stakes domains, often presenting tool-using agents as a natural next step beyond traditional programming and clinical decision support. Many scientists and engineers who are not steeped in the details of generative modeling accept these proposals at face value or propose agentic systems of their own. The growing enthusiasm for ever more agentic systems is therefore not just philosophically troubling; it is a concrete safety concern to the extent that it ignores this structural constraint.

Consequence (architectural remedy): if one accepts this mathematical constraint, the way to avoid orchestration hallucination is not to demand more elaborate reasoning traces from the agent, but to take generative models out of the orchestration loop. Execution must be restricted to a set of prevalidated, deterministic workflows, and large language models must be used only as translators from human needs and clinical narratives to formal intents that select among those workflows. I describe an Intent–Action Service that compiles each intent to a single canonical workflow, collapsing the combinatorial action space to one path per intent and localizing residual uncertainty to the interpretation of user intent rather than to the construction of procedures. I argue that for clinical and other safety-critical domains, this separation between probabilistic interpretation and deterministic execution is not merely advisable but required.

My motivation in writing this paper is straightforward: to make explicit, with simple mathematics rather than opinion, why agentic orchestration is structurally unsafe and why deterministic alternatives are necessary. Claims about ever more agentic systems in healthcare and other high-stakes settings are accelerating; readers deserve a clear account of the risks and a reasoned argument for keeping AI out of the execution loop.

== Introduction

``Lies, damned lies, and statistics"footnote:[Often attributed to Mark Twain, possibly originally Benjamin Disraeli.] is a reminder that numbers can be made to serve any story. If statistics can bend, so can its sibling: probability. It lets us say, “the truth is out there, and most likely in this _region_,” when pinning a single point is hard or impossible. Probability was supposed to help us grapple with complex, subsampled phenomena. A 95% chance of rain tomorrow was never meant to mean it _will_ rain. Many seem to forget that probability is the engine in every computational atom of generative AI, dazzling with an appearance of determinism. Sometimes that “good enough” shine hides unacceptable risk, especially in systems that touch human health and well-being. If a self-driving car crashes 1% of the time, the argument that it's definitely correct the other 99% of the time is small comfort.

In 1980 Searle argued in his seminal ``Minds, Brain, and Programs" paper against the late‑1970s idea that a suitably programmed computer (what he called "strong AI") would literally have a mind or reveal how human minds work pass:[\cite{searle1980}]. His point was that mere manipulation of symbols according to rules (_syntax_) is not the same as grasping their meaning (_semantics_). Anthropomorphism blinds, and shortcuts that produce good answers can fool us into ascribing understanding where there is none. Almost half a century later we have generative AI systems that are still syntactic engines, now implemented as probabilistic pattern machines in silicon. They move probability mass over a space of token sequences but do not, by themselves, come any closer to meaning or to _truth_. The probability machinery that powers the generative step also complicates determinism and reproducibility compared with Searle’s idealized lookup tables, yet it does not weaken his thesis; if anything, the probabilistic twist makes these machines less suitable in contexts where human well‑being is at stake. Certain voices nevertheless argue that such systems can be chained together to construct analyses in any context, including medical ones. But just because their outputs _seem_ correct, or are highly probable under the model, does not mean they _are_ correct in the sense that matters for patients. The appearance of correctness tells us nothing about the underlying, reproducible, deterministic truth we actually need.

As Searle challenged the claims about AI current in his time, I want here to challenge what now threatens to become a similarly entrenched and oddly underexamined assumption: that agentic computation, using LLMs not merely to emit text but to assemble programs and orchestrate complex analyses on the fly, is a sound and sensible use of the technology. Where Searle, the consummate philosopher, leaned on a ``Chinese room" Gedankenexperiment, I will lean on simple mathematics, as befits my academic roots in engineering and physical sciences.

Of course “lies" and “hallucinations" are anthropomorphisms. A probabilistic sampler has no idea what a lie is (just as it has no ideas of any kind); it just draws a sample from a cloud. The _semantic_ meaning is assigned by the external observer (us) who decide whether that draw lands inside or outside the tiny sliver of acceptable outcomes. Searle’s man in the Chinese room was not lying about Chinese; he was following a lookup. LLMs do the same. In safety-critical domains the space of “wrong” is enormous and the space of “right” is tiny, so with non-zero probability on the wrong side agents will always “lie” in the only sense that matters: they will inevitably produce unacceptable outputs. Attempts to fix this with ever more tools and longer “reasoning traces” may reduce some failure modes, but they do not remove the structural uncertainty created when probabilistic generation is allowed to drive execution.

Agentic generative AI has rapidly moved from a research curiosity to an asserted solution for complex, high-stakes clinical tasks. The prevailing narrative is that a large language model (LLM), equipped with institutional data, publications, and tools, can function as an “AI clinician” or autonomous assistant: given a patient history and imaging findings, the system will propose diagnoses, prognoses, or clinical workflows pass:[\cite{brown2020,rajpurkar2022aihealth,sendak2020translation}]. Yet empirical studies show persistent hallucination and faithfulness gaps even under constraints and guardrails pass:[\cite{ji2023survey,maynez2020faithfulness,liu2024faithfulness,bender2021parrots,manakul2023selfcheckgpt,lin2022truthfulqa,pagnoni2021factuality}]. Underspecification and distribution shift further undermine reproducibility: models with identical training loss can diverge on real-world data pass:[\cite{damour2020underspec,recht2019imagenet}]. Multiple recent papers argue that hallucination is structurally inevitable for LLMs and agents, even with improved prompting or architectural tweaks pass:[\cite{zhou2024hallucinate,chen2024inevitable,openai2025whyhallucinate,lin2025agentsurvey}]. A large 2026 agent survey now describes the field as moving from passive language models to goal-directed systems with perception, planning, tool use, and collaboration, while explicitly listing “hallucination in action,” infinite loops, and prompt injection as open problems pass:[\cite{arunkumar2026agentic}]. Tool-augmented agents such as OpenAI’s Model Context Protocol (MCP) now provide standardized registries and invocation semantics for external tools, encouraging LLMs to assemble multi-step procedures at runtime pass:[\cite{openai2024mcp,openai2024assistants}]. MCP lowers integration friction but also enlarges the action graph that an agent can traverse, pushing the field toward increasingly agentic orchestration in clinical and enterprise settings pass:[\cite{anthropic2024tooluse}]. At the same time, new open-world evaluations report that tool-using agents struggle with large tool pools, long-horizon objectives, wild constraints, and unreliable tool states pass:[\cite{xi2026toolgym}]. Even outside language-only settings, 2026 work on multimodal agents treats “agentic depth” itself as a systems bottleneck severe enough to warrant speculative acceleration frameworks pass:[\cite{huang2026speceyes}]. And failure-focused studies of foundation-model decision making continue to show that strong average task success can coexist with consequential breakdowns on safety-relevant cases pass:[\cite{han2026beforetrust}].

Over roughly the past year this trajectory has accelerated. The literature is no longer asking only whether agents can be built; it is classifying agent architectures, benchmarking them in larger open-world environments, and reducing the latency costs of deeper tool-and-planning loops pass:[\cite{arunkumar2026agentic,xi2026toolgym,huang2026speceyes}]. Vendor roadmaps, technical blogs, and conference talks now routinely present tool-using agents as the natural next step after classical programming and static clinical decision support, and pilot systems are described as if agentic orchestration were an obviously reasonable evolution rather than a substantive change in risk profile. Many scientists and engineers who are not steeped in the details of generative modeling accept these claims, or propose agentic workflows of their own, without fully grappling with the probabilistic limits just described.

A common claim in this space is that hallucination can be controlled by forcing explicit intermediate outputs. If the system is required to emit reasoning chains, code, or deterministic-looking programs, and if it operates over a sufficiently rich evidence space, then the hope is that errors become rare and easily detectable pass:[\cite{xu2024impossible,chen2023pal,openai2024mitigating}]. This narrative is appealing, particularly to non-technical audiences; it suggests that transparency of steps naturally leads to correctness.

This paper argues that this view is fundamentally mistaken, or at least dangerously incomplete. The problem is not intermediate structure as such, but self-generated intermediate structure. When a model is asked to reduce hallucination by emitting more plans, code, or reasoning traces, the system risks creating an epistemic echo chamber in which model-produced text is treated as evidence for the correctness of other model-produced text. Intermediate representations become genuine grounding only when they are produced, updated, or validated by mechanisms external to the generative policy itself: executed tools, materialized artifacts, state receipts, logs, hashes, or other independently governed substrates. Hallucination is not a superficial defect of generative models that can be removed through better prompting or insistence on explicit steps. It is a structural property of probabilistic systems operating over a very large space of possible action sequences pass:[\cite{vaswani2017,ji2023survey,maynez2020faithfulness}]. When the number of exposed tools increases and the number of generated reasoning steps grows, the opportunities for deviation multiply.

The key bridge to orchestration is simple. A next-token distribution, by itself, is only a probabilistic text generator. It becomes an orchestration mechanism when some of those generated tokens are interpreted as executable control variables: which tool to call, which argument to bind, which branch to take, which code to run, which state transition to authorize. At that point, decoding is no longer merely selecting words; it is inducing transitions in an external state machine. Runtime orchestration is therefore not separate from generation. It is generation whose outputs have been granted procedural force. Once that move is made, the relevant object of risk is no longer only an incorrect sentence but an incorrect path through an executable action graph.

To make this precise, we develop a graph-theoretic formulation of the action space available to an agent. The executable surface exposed to a tool-using model is naturally represented as a directed graph: tools or operational states define vertices, admissible transitions define edges, and concrete workflows are paths through that graph. Safety then depends not merely on whether a tool is reachable, but on whether the realized path belongs to a much smaller set of clinically valid paths. We show that the number of possible workflows grows combinatorially with the number of tools and the length of the procedure, and that any probabilistic policy over this space has a non-zero probability of selecting an invalid workflow under mild assumptions about support on invalid paths. Requiring more detailed reasoning does not, by itself, solve this problem; it often creates additional opportunities for deviation. It is no small irony that the attempt to expose more of the system’s internally generated structure, as a means of improving reliability, can make the outputs more suspect, not less.

We then introduce the concept of an Intent–Action Service (IAS). IAS is a deterministic compiler from high-level clinical intents to validated executable actions. In the simplest case, a single intent compiles to a complete canonical workflow, so that the system maps each intent to a single, prevalidated path through the tool graph. More generally, in multi-step settings, IAS can operate stage by stage: at each externally materialized system state, an intent is mapped to the uniquely valid next action or subworkflow permitted from that state. Under either construction, the dangerous procedural space is collapsed not by asking the agent to narrate more of it, but by binding execution to a deterministic map external to the agent itself. The agent may invoke this map, but it does not define it. Likewise, the state that counts as reality is not asserted by the agent’s own outputs; it is materialized by external deterministic operations, receipts, or artifacts that the agent can call upon but not authoritatively invent. This separation of generative interpretation from deterministic execution provides a way to localize hallucination and to bound where it can affect clinical systems.

The remainder of this paper develops the theoretical formulation, contrasts agentic and IAS-based architectures, and outlines an experimental design to evaluate their behavior in a controlled imaging environment.

== Why Generative Models Are Probabilistic

=== The Deterministic Underpinning

The core computational objects underlying modern generative AI are artificial neural networks. Historically, these constructions were loosely inspired by simplified early abstractions of biological neurons pass:[\cite{mcculloch1943}]footnote:[There is a striking historical inversion here. The original McCulloch-Pitts construction was introduced as a simplification of nervous activity, not as a biologically faithful model of neural tissue. Modern neural networks are vastly more elaborate, but elaboration is not ontological revision. The basic object remains a fully tractable mathematical surrogate. Nevertheless, contemporary discourse often treats scale and benchmark performance as if they had converted the abstraction into the thing abstracted, and sometimes as if that abstraction could surpass its biological referent on that basis alone. No empirical result or theoretical argument currently licenses that conclusion. Performance gains do not, by themselves, justify a metaphysical promotion.], but in modern machine learning they are best understood as parameterized mathematical maps composed of affine transformations and nonlinearities. Their role in this paper is not biological analogy but mathematical structure: with fixed parameters, a neural network implements a deterministic forward transformation from one vector space to another. Put plainly, once the weights are fixed and the input is fixed, the output of the forward pass is fixed as well. The same input presented to the same network in inference mode yields the same output every time. In many practically relevant settings, this forward map is effectively compressive on the data distribution: a high-dimensional input is sent to a more compact representation, and the image of the data under that map often concentrates on a lower-dimensional manifold or structured subset of representation space.

.Schematic bias-free stem:[4 \to 3 \to 2] feedforward network used as a concrete illustration of the deterministic forward map. The essential features for the present argument are the directed, mechanical forward sweep and the compression from four scalar inputs to two scalar outputs.

To make the discussion concrete, consider a simple bias-free stem:[4 \to 3 \to 2] network with inputs stem:[x_1,\dots,x_4], hidden activations stem:[h_1,h_2,h_3], and outputs stem:[z_1,z_2]. I use bold lowercase symbols for vectors, bold uppercase symbols for matrices, and calligraphic symbols for spaces and sets; scalar quantities are written in ordinary italic type. Let stem:[W_{ij}] denote the weights from input stem:[i] to hidden unit stem:[j], let stem:[V_{jk}] denote the weights from hidden unit stem:[j] to output stem:[k], and let stem:[\sigma(t) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}] be a sigmoid nonlinearity. Then

[stem]
++++
h_j = \sigma\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{4} W_{ij} x_i \right), \qquad j=1,2,3,
++++

and

[stem]
++++
z_k = \sigma\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3} V_{jk} h_j \right), \qquad k=1,2.
++++

Substituting for the hidden activations gives the explicit forward map

[stem]
++++
z_k = \sigma\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{3} V_{jk}\,\sigma\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{4} W_{ij} x_i \right)\right), \qquad k=1,2.
++++

Equivalently, in vector form, let stem:[\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 4}], stem:[\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 3}], and stem:[\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 2}], with stem:[\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 3}] and stem:[\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 2}]. Then the same forward pass can be written compactly as

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{h} = \sigma(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W}), \qquad \mathbf{z} = \sigma(\mathbf{h}\mathbf{V}),
++++

or, by composition,

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{z} = \sigma\!\big(\sigma(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{W})\mathbf{V}\big),
++++

where stem:[\sigma] acts componentwise.

This expression is nonlinear, but it is entirely explicit, mechanical, and **deterministic** once the weights are fixed. That is the key point: the forward pass is not mysterious, not interpretive, and not stochastic. It is simply a composition of weighted sums and pointwise nonlinearities. The crucial structural feature, however, is not nonlinearity by itself but **compression**: the network maps high-dimensional input degrees of freedom to lower-dimensional output coordinates through successive non-square layers. One might incorrectly infer that being fully deterministic means different inputs give different outputs, i.e.

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{x}_1 \neq \mathbf{x}_2 \;\Longrightarrow\; g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_1) \neq g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_2).
++++

However, determinism does not mean uniqueness. In reality the following is typically unavoidable:

[stem]
++++
\exists\, \mathbf{x}_1 \neq \mathbf{x}_2 \quad \text{such that} \quad g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_1) = g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_2),
++++

or, more realistically on the data distribution, stem:[g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_1) \approx g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_2)]. This is exactly what it means for the forward map to be non-injective in general. Determinism in the forward direction therefore does not imply recoverability in the reverse direction: the same representation may be compatible with many distinct antecedents, so the output stem:[\mathbf{z}] no longer uniquely determines its source stem:[\mathbf{x}].

Abstracting from this concrete stem:[4 \to 3 \to 2] example, we can summarize the forward map as follows: let stem:[\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d] denote the input space and stem:[\mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k] the output, or representation, space of the forward map, with stem:[k \ll d] in the compressive regime of interest here. Let stem:[g_{\theta} : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Z}] denote the deterministic forward map implemented by a network with fixed parameters stem:[\theta], so that

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{z} = g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) .
++++


=== The Probabilistic Inverse

Intuitively, the obstacle is information loss: in the compressive regime stem:[k \ll d], multiple distinct inputs can map to the same representation, so the forward map is generally non-injective and exact inversion is unavailable. One can still ask whether one might move back from stem:[\mathbf{z}] to stem:[\mathbf{x}] by an inverse map stem:[g_{\theta}^{-1}], i.e.

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{x} = g_{\theta}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) .
++++

In general, however, this is not possible. For a given representation stem:[\mathbf{z}], the preimage

[stem]
++++
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z}) = \{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X} \mid g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{z} \}
++++

is large for a typical stem:[\mathbf{z}]. In the concrete stem:[4 \to 3 \to 2] example above, this is the familiar fact that two output coordinates cannot, in general, uniquely recover four input coordinates: the forward map is compressive and therefore non-injective. There is therefore no unique antecedent to recover. The only meaningful reverse object is not a function stem:[g_{\theta}^{-1}(\mathbf{z})] but a conditional distribution stem:[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})] whose support lies in the compatible preimage set, i.e. stem:[\operatorname{supp}(p_{\theta}(\cdot \mid \mathbf{z})) \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})]. Generative modeling is the mechanical process of parameterizing such a conditional distribution pass:[\cite{brown2020,vaswani2017,kingma2013vae}]. Sampling then draws one member of the class:

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}).
++++

Any decoding rule, whether stochastic (temperature/top-k/top-p) or even greedy argmax, chooses an element from the compatible set rather than inverting stem:[g_{\theta}] to a single input. Determinism would require collapsing stem:[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})] to a single point, which sacrifices coverage and fidelity. Because the mapping stem:[\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{z}] is many-to-one, the reverse direction is inherently probabilistic.

== Generative AI and Predictive AI

It is helpful to distinguish two very different uses of the same underlying machinery. In classical predictive AI, a neural network is used in its forward sense: a fixed input is mapped, by a deterministic forward pass, to a label, score, segmentation, embedding, or other task-specific output. In medical imaging, familiar examples include classifying a chest radiograph, detecting suspicious lesions on mammography, segmenting a brain tumor or organ boundary on MRI, estimating a ventricular volume or ejection fraction on echocardiography, or assigning a hemorrhage-risk score from head CT. Even when architectures contain encoder-decoder structure, as in U-Nets, the computation remains a forward evaluation of a deterministic map. The network may downsample and later upsample, but it is still computing a fixed output from a fixed input by a prescribed sequence of operations. In that sense, traditional predictive use treats the neural network as a deterministic transformation of information already present in the input.

Generative AI uses the same kind of deterministic forward computation internally, but for a different mathematical purpose. It is not primarily trying to map a rich input to a compact decision or representation. Instead, it is asked to expand a comparatively small, partial, or compressed condition into a larger output such as text, code, or an image. In that sense, generative modeling addresses a reverse-direction problem: not the forward compression of many details into a representation, but the production of one admissible realization from a space of many possibilities compatible with that representation or context.

That distinction is crucial. A compressive map can be deterministic even when it is many-to-one. But once many distinct antecedents are compatible with the same representation, there is no unique object to recover in the reverse direction. Generation therefore cannot, in general, be a true inverse of the forward map. It must instead parameterize a conditional distribution over compatible outputs and then select, sample, or decode one member of that set.

Large language models make this especially clear. The network itself still performs an ordinary deterministic forward pass for a given context and parameter setting. But the result of that forward pass is not a single truth-preserving recovery of what must come next. It is a probability distribution over possible next tokens, and multi-token generation is built by repeatedly selecting from such distributions. The probabilistic character of the system is therefore not an implementation detail layered on top of an otherwise deterministic mechanism. It is intrinsic to the generative task itself.

This is why generative AI, including LLMs, cannot be treated as if it were merely a larger or more sophisticated version of predictive inference. Predictive systems can be embedded inside deterministic workflows because they compute bounded task outputs from given inputs. Generative systems, by contrast, are asked to expand underdetermined conditions into one of many admissible outputs. That expansion is inherently probabilistic. For that reason, a generative model can be useful as an interpreter, translator, or proposal engine, but it cannot serve as a guaranteed truth-preserving orchestrator of safety-critical procedures.

== Why Exact Inversion Does Not Rescue Correctness

The argument above establishes why the generative step is probabilistic. But even if one sets that point aside and asks whether exact inversion would solve the correctness problem, the answer is still no. The issue is not merely that a representation stem:[\mathbf{z}] admits many compatible antecedents. It is also that only a small subset of those antecedents are semantically, clinically, or procedurally acceptable.

For a given representation stem:[\mathbf{z}], define the compatible preimage set

[stem]
++++
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{z}) = \{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X} \mid g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathbf{z} \,\} .
++++

This is the approximate counterpart of stem:[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{z})], relaxing exact equality to neighborhood-level compatibility in representation space.

A parameterized model defines a conditional distribution stem:[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})] supported on stem:[\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{z})]. Within stem:[\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{z})], only a small observer-validated subset stem:[\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{z}) \subset \mathcal{S}(\mathbf{z})] corresponds to clinically acceptable reconstructions.

The probability mass assigned to clinically acceptable reconstructions for stem:[\mathbf{z}] is

[stem]
++++
P_{\text{correct}}(\mathbf{z}) = \int_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{G}(\mathbf{z})} p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})\, d\mathbf{x} ,
++++

and stem:[1 - P_{\text{correct}}(\mathbf{z})] is the mass on antecedents that are mathematically admissible or probabilistically plausible but clinically wrong. This point is logically independent of the inverse problem itself: even a deterministic selection rule over stem:[\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{z})] would not guarantee that the selected element lies in stem:[\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{z})].

Full inversion of stem:[g] would not rescue correctness either. Enforcing bijectivity would eliminate compression, preserve noise, and force the model toward a degenerate edge case: a reversible lookup that cannot reduce dimension, cannot discard nuisance variation, and cannot extrapolate beyond its training support. Invertible flow families (e.g., NICE/Real NVP/Glow) explore this corner of the space but still rely on sampling and do not solve semantic correctness pass:[\cite{dinh2017realnvp,kingma2018glow}]. Two distinct patients with the same diagnosis would not map to a single clinically meaningful reconstruction; a deterministic inverse would therefore be misleading even if it were mathematically available.

In practice, this non-injectivity is by design. Modern architectures routinely compress representations through lower-dimensional intermediate spaces, so any inverse-like operation must act over a preimage set rather than recover a single exact antecedent.

Therefore, hallucination risk is structural at two levels: the mapping from prompts to outputs is underdetermined, and only a subset of the admissible output space is clinically valid. Probabilistic sampling is inherent, but even hypothetical exact inversion would not restore correctness. Determinism belongs in orchestration (IAS), not in the generative core.

At first glance, encoder-decoder architectures such as U-Nets may seem to complicate this argument pass:[\cite{ronneberger2015unet}]footnote:[Here I refer to U-Nets in their standard discriminative encoder-decoder role, not to their reuse as components within generative diffusion pipelines.]. They downsample and later upsample, and so may appear to offer a deterministic analogue of inversion.

They do not. In common discriminative uses such as segmentation, the upsampling path remains a deterministic feedforward computation conditioned not only on bottleneck features but also on skip-connected high-resolution features from the encoder. The decoder is therefore not selecting one antecedent from a preimage set; it is computing a task-specific output from retained input information. U-Nets are therefore not counterexamples to the distinction drawn here between deterministic forward computation and probabilistic inverse-like generation.

== Agents, Orchestration, and the Inevitable Risk of Depth

An agent, in the sense relevant to this paper, is not merely a language model that produces text. It is a generative model whose outputs are treated as executable decisions: code to be run, tools to be invoked, parameters to be filled, or workflow steps to be chosen. This is precisely the shift made explicit in reasoning-and-acting frameworks such as ReAct and in later tool-use/orchestration surveys: model output is no longer only descriptive, but operational pass:[\cite{yao2023react,lin2025agentsurvey,xu2026tooluse}]. Once model output is materialized in this way, hallucination is no longer just a linguistic defect. It becomes procedural. As in Searle’s critique, syntax can be made to simulate competence without supplying understanding. In the agentic setting, however, the issue is no longer merely philosophical: syntax is now being materialized as action still with no understanding of semantics.

This is the architectural mistake at the center of current agentic enthusiasm. A large language model is asked not merely to interpret a problem, but to control a sequence of dependent actions. That is an entirely different use of the model. It places a probabilistic generator inside an execution loop. In MCP-style settings, the primitives are exposed explicitly; in code-generating settings, they are composed implicitly through synthesized programs. But the structure is the same: a generative model is placed inside a loop whose outputs determine executable steps. Studies of multi-step agent use already identify planning and tool-employment as key bottlenecks even in narrower domains such as database question answering, underscoring that the difficulty is not only in final answer generation but in the dependent action chain itself pass:[\cite{nan2023reasoningaction}]. In healthcare, this agentic framing is already being promoted as a natural architectural next step, which is precisely why the mathematical limits discussed here matter so urgently pass:[\cite{badr2026agentic}].

Generative models are often useful at breadth. They can translate ambiguous user language, surface candidate interpretations, summarize contexts, and map messy narrative input to a smaller set of admissible intents. But orchestration is a problem of depth: a chain of executable choices in which each step constrains the next. In such a setting, a non-zero probability of error at any one step implies a non-zero probability of procedural failure somewhere in the chain. Additional steps do not repair this weakness; they expose it repeatedly. A wrong tool, a wrong parameter, an omitted step, or a wrongly generated program are all instances of the same underlying phenomenon: probabilistic generation has been asked to serve as procedural control. The point is especially clear in code generation, where fluent outputs can still contain logical, robustness, or security defects despite appearing locally plausible pass:[\cite{agarwal2024codemirage}].

This depth problem is naturally described in graph-theoretic terms. Once a system exposes tools, commands, or executable subroutines, it exposes a directed action graph. Each decision point presents outgoing continuations; each multi-step workflow is a path; and each additional generated step extends the path while creating another opportunity to leave the region of clinically acceptable behavior. In simple cases one may picture a clean acyclic workflow diagram, but the argument does not depend on acyclicity. Retries, revisitation, polling, and backtracking only strengthen the point by creating more decision points and more opportunities for drift.

One can already see, at an intuitive level, why external grounding helps without fully solving the problem. If a system repeatedly materializes state outside the model and restricts admissible next actions to those compatible with that state, then the effective local branching factor is reduced. The model is no longer roaming freely over the full reachable graph. This can reduce local risk substantially. But unless the remaining transition from intent to execution is itself deterministic, the local hazard is reduced rather than eliminated. That intermediate regime matters in practice, but it does not invalidate the central claim: as long as a probabilistic model still controls executable branching, non-zero orchestration risk remains.

The consequence is immediate. If one wants to minimize execution error, then one should minimize the number of generative choices that are allowed to affect execution. In the limit, this pushes toward a single hop: from user expression to formal intent, and then stop. Procedure construction, tool ordering, parameter binding, and execution should pass to deterministic software.

What this means in practice is not an autonomous planner but a large, explicit switchboard of deterministic, prevalidated choices. Workflows, tool orderings, admissible parameters, and execution paths must be specified, reviewed, and governed in advance. The generative model may help map user language onto this controlled space, but it must not be allowed to invent the space at runtime.

We formalize and name this architecture, in which generative breadth is used only to map user expression to a selected workflow-bearing intent, as an Intent–Action Service (IAS). In the simplest case, IAS maps one intent to one full canonical workflow. In staged settings, the same principle can be applied repeatedly: at each externally materialized state, the model may help interpret user expression, but a deterministic map resolves that intent to the next valid action or subworkflow. IAS does not make generation safer by placing another guardrail around orchestration; it removes generation from orchestration itself. The model may choose the intent, but it must not construct the procedure, and it must not define the state that counts as execution truth. The remainder of the paper makes this claim precise by modeling agentic orchestration as a probability distribution over workflow paths, and IAS as a deterministic collapse of that space to one valid path per intent or per state-conditioned decision point.

== Theoretical Formulation of the Action Space

The central object in our analysis is the space of possible tool-level workflows that a system may execute. We model this space as a directed graph and characterize the behavior of an agent as a probability distribution over paths in that graph.

Between the two limiting cases of unconstrained agentic orchestration and fully deterministic IAS lies an important intermediate regime. In its purest form, IAS collapses execution to a single selection hop: a high-level intent is mapped to one canonical workflow. For sufficiently complex or state-sensitive workflows, however, choosing the entire downstream procedure in one shot may itself become an error-prone act of selection. In such cases, the same architectural principle can be applied iteratively. External deterministic state is materialized after each step, and the next intent-to-action mapping is resolved from that state. The action space is then not collapsed once globally, but collapsed repeatedly and locally at each branching point. Such systems reduce orchestration hazard relative to unconstrained MCP-style agents, while generally failing to drive that hazard to zero unless the remaining mapping from state and intent to action is itself deterministic. We introduce the base MCP and IAS formulations first, and return later to this middle regime as a corollary.

For clarity of notation, we use a single notion of orchestration error. For an intent stem:[i], let stem:[H_{MCP}(i)] denote the probability that the agent induces a workflow lying outside the clinically valid path set. When we later introduce explicit blocked or failure states, they are simply another representation of this same event: the induced path has left the valid region of the workflow graph. We write stem:[H_{MCP}(i; L)] for the probability of at least one orchestration error in a length-stem:[L] workflow, stem:[P_{\text{valid}}(i; L)] for the probability that all steps in such a workflow remain valid, and stem:[H_{IAS}(i)] for the corresponding quantity under IAS.

=== Tool Graph and Workflow Paths

Let stem:[G = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})] be a directed graph of tools. The vertex set stem:[\mathcal{V}] represents the primitive operations that can be invoked, for example “create experiment starting with data stem:[x],” “run analysis steps stem:[A_i i\in{1..k}] in specific order,” “perform quality check stem:[Q],”, “export results  stem:[E]”, etc The edge set stem:[\mathcal{E} \subseteq \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{V}] encodes allowed transitions, that is, which tool may validly follow which other tool.

For example, the above hypothetical could be expressed as

[stem]
++++
s = A_1 \rightarrow A_2 \rightarrow ... A_k \rightarrow Q \rightarrow E
++++

where logically the specific choice of say stem:[A_i] constrains the set of valid stem:[A_{i+1}]. In general, a workflow is a finite path in stem:[G]. We write

[stem]
++++
s = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_L), \quad v_k \in \mathcal{V},\; (v_k, v_{k+1}) \in \mathcal{E} ,
++++

where stem:[L] is the length of the workflow.

Let stem:[\mathcal{S}(G)] denote the set of all reachable paths in stem:[G]. Among these, only a proper and typically very small subset corresponds to clinically valid workflows. We denote this subset by stem:[\mathcal{S}_{valid} \subsetneq \mathcal{S}(G)], where each element satisfies domain-specific constraints such as correct tool selection, correct order, presence of required steps, and adherence to safety rules. In the regimes of interest here, one typically has stem:[|\mathcal{S}_{valid}| \ll |\mathcal{S}(G)|]. The invalid path set is then defined by the set difference

[stem]
++++
\mathcal{S}_{invalid} := \mathcal{S}(G) \setminus \mathcal{S}_{valid}
++++

collects all workflows that are reachable in the tool graph but clinically invalid. A hallucination in orchestration is precisely the event of selecting a path stem:[s \in \mathcal{S}_{invalid}].

This formulation is intentionally agnostic to the details of the tools. It abstracts away the semantics of individual operations and concentrates only on the structure of possible compositions.

=== Agent Policy over Workflows

Consider some intent stem:[i], which in a clinical computational setting could be “run MRI segmentation pipeline stem:[P] on dataset stem:[D].” Here a _pipeline_ is an ordered set of computations expressed in a DAG structrue. An MCP-style agent, or any agent accessing tools through a registry, does not usually select a complete workflow in one shot. Rather, it makes a sequence of runtime decisions whose composition induces a conditional probability distribution over complete workflow paths stem:[s \in \mathcal{S}(G)] for the given intent. We write this induced path distribution as

[stem]
++++
\pi_{MCP}(s \mid i), \quad s \in \mathcal{S}(G) .
++++

In other words, stem:[\pi_{MCP}(s \mid i)] is the probability that the agent's sequential runtime behavior yields the complete executable path stem:[s], given intent stem:[i]. The orchestration hallucination probability for intent stem:[i] is then the total probability mass assigned to invalid paths:

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
H_{MCP}(i)
&= \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{invalid}} \pi_{MCP}(s \mid i) \\
&= 1 - \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}_{valid}} \pi_{MCP}(s \mid i) .
\end{aligned}
++++

Unless stem:[\pi_{MCP}(\cdot \mid i)] places nonzero probability only on stem:[\mathcal{S}_{valid}], we have stem:[H_{MCP}(i) > 0]. In practice, stem:[\mathcal{S}_{invalid}] is combinatorially enormous, and there is no realistic mechanism for forcing stem:[\pi_{MCP}] to place nonzero probability only on stem:[\mathcal{S}_{valid}] without eliminating runtime freedom in workflow assembly.

This is the whole point: hallucination is not an occasional glitch in agentic orchestration. It is the generic outcome of letting a probabilistic policy range over a large executable space that contains bad paths as well as good ones. And as workflows become longer, the number of decision points and admissible continuations increases, creating more opportunities for the induced path to drift into error. In that sense, deeper orchestration does not merely preserve risk; it compounds it.

Although we formulate this first in terms of tool and workflow selection, the same logic extends to any agentic intermediate process whose outputs carry procedural force. Once generated “reasoning” is allowed to determine what happens next, it too becomes part of the induced path through action space.

=== Combinatorial Size of the Space

The previous subsection gives the core inevitability condition: unless the policy places nonzero probability only on valid workflows, the orchestration hallucination probability is strictly positive. The present subsection is more illustrative than foundational. Its purpose is to show why that non-zero risk becomes practically severe as workflows grow longer and the reachable action space expands. For simplicity, assume that at each step the agent can choose, on average, from stem:[d] outgoing tools, and that workflows have typical length stem:[L]. Under these assumptions, the number of possible length-stem:[L] paths is on the order of

[stem]
++++
|\mathcal{S}_L(G)| \approx d^L .
++++

For illustration, consider a simplified six-stage imaging workflow: convert a DICOM series into a model-ready working image representation, normalize intensities, run an inference model to estimate tumor volume, project the result back into image space, repackage the derived output into DICOM-compatible form, and transmit it to the correct clinical study record. Even this deliberately simplified pipeline contains multiple opportunities to choose the wrong tool, bind the wrong parameter, operate on the wrong series, use the wrong normalization regime, attach the output to the wrong study, or omit a required transformation. If each stage presents only stem:[d = 3] plausible continuations, then there are already stem:[3^6 = 729] possible paths through the six-stage workflow. If the effective branching factor is stem:[d = 5], the path count rises to stem:[5^6 = 15{,}625]. And if one considers a slightly deeper eight-stage workflow with stem:[d = 4], then stem:[|\mathcal{S}_8(G)| \approx 4^8 = 65{,}536]. In realistic clinical settings, only a tiny fraction of these reachable paths would correspond to semantically and procedurally correct behavior.

Even if the clinically valid subset stem:[\mathcal{S}_{valid,L} \subset \mathcal{S}_L(G)] is small, the agent’s policy stem:[\pi_{MCP}] must operate over a space whose size grows exponentially in stem:[L]. If we think of a per-step mis-selection probability stem:[\varepsilon > 0], representing the chance of an incorrect tool assignment, an invalid transition, or omission of a mandatory step at each decision point, then the probability that a length-stem:[L] workflow is fully correct is crudely approximated by

[stem]
++++
\mathbb{P}[\text{all steps correct}] \approx (1 - \varepsilon)^L,
++++

so that the probability of at least one orchestration error is

[stem]
++++
H_{MCP}(i; L) \approx 1 - (1 - \varepsilon)^L .
++++

For an eight-stage workflow stem:[(L = 8)], even a modest per-step orchestration error rate accumulates quickly. If stem:[\varepsilon = 0.01], then the per-step correctness probability is stem:[1 - \varepsilon = 0.99], so stem:[H_{MCP}(i;8) \approx 1 - (1 - 0.01)^8 = 1 - 0.99^8 \approx 0.077], or about 7.7%. At stem:[\varepsilon = 0.02], stem:[H_{MCP}(i;8) \approx 14.9\%]. At stem:[\varepsilon = 0.05], stem:[H_{MCP}(i;8) \approx 33.7\%]. These risks are incurred over a path space that already contains stem:[3^8 = 6{,}561] possible workflows when stem:[d = 3], and stem:[5^8 = 390{,}625] when stem:[d = 5].

This quantity increases with stem:[L]. The graph-space penalty is exact: the larger the tool graph and the longer the workflows, the harder it becomes to avoid orchestration hallucinations. More tools and more steps, even with additional reasoning, do not shrink this space; they expand it.

The simplified picture above assumes a roughly constant branching factor and the simplifying assumption that each step has the same independent error rate. Real tool graphs are more heterogeneous: some regions are skinny and tightly constrained, others are wide and loosely constrained. We now refine the analysis to account for this without changing the central conclusion: as long as there exists any reachable decision point with non-zero local error, the overall orchestration hallucination probability can never be driven to zero.

=== Heterogeneous Graph Structure and Local Error Rates

At this point a natural counterargument is that the previous analysis was too simple. Real workflow graphs are not homogeneous, real decision points are not equally risky, and real systems often contain narrow, well-constrained regions in which error rates may be very small. This is exactly the objection to examine. The purpose of the present refinement is to show that the central conclusion does not depend on the crude assumptions of the previous subsection. Even when local risk varies across the graph, any reachable non-zero local error implies non-zero global orchestration risk.

To make the role of graph structure explicit, let stem:[h_t = (v_1, \dots, v_t)] denote the prefix (history) of a workflow at step stem:[t]. In other words, if a full workflow is a path through the tool graph, then stem:[h_t] is the partial path traced out up to time stem:[t]. Some such prefixes are still “on track” in the sense that they can still be extended into at least one clinically valid full workflow, while others have already left the region from which a clinically valid completion remains possible. Among these prefixes, define the set of valid prefixes

[stem]
++++
R_t = \{\, h_t \mid h_t \text{ is reachable under } \pi_{MCP}(\cdot \mid i),\; h_t \text{ is a prefix of some } s \in \mathcal{S}_{valid} \,\} .
++++

Intuitively, stem:[R_t] collects the partial workflows that are still on track to become clinically valid. At each valid prefix stem:[h_t \in R_t], we can then ask a more local question than before: given that the agent is still on a valid track at step stem:[t], what is the chance that its very next move will move the workflow outside the valid region? This leads to the local orchestration error rate

[stem]
++++
\varepsilon_t(h_t)
= \mathbb{P}\big[ h_{t+1} \notin R_{t+1} \,\big|\, h_t,\; h_t \in R_t \big] .
++++

This quantity already folds in both graph shape and policy behavior. In a skinny, well-structured region with strong constraints and good policies, stem:[\varepsilon_t(h_t)] may be very small or even exactly zero. In a wide, loosely constrained region, or under a poorly calibrated policy, stem:[\varepsilon_t(h_t)] will typically be larger.

Let stem:[P_{\text{valid}}(i; L)] denote the probability that a length-stem:[L] workflow for intent stem:[i] remains valid at every step. For any particular realized history, this probability is obtained by multiplying the local survival terms stem:[(1 - \varepsilon_t(h_t))] across the successive steps. Conditioning on staying valid up to each time stem:[t], the probability chain rule therefore gives a product of stepwise conditional survival probabilities. Since the realized history is itself random under stem:[\pi_{MCP}(\cdot \mid i)], the overall validity probability is the expectation stem:[\mathbb{E}], that is, the average of this product over the histories induced by the policy:

[stem]
++++
P_{\text{valid}}(i; L)
= \mathbb{E}\Big[ \prod_{t=1}^{L-1} (1 - \varepsilon_t(h_t)) \Big] ,
++++

where the expectation is over random histories stem:[h_t] induced by stem:[\pi_{MCP}(\cdot \mid i)] and restricted to valid prefixes. The corresponding probability of at least one orchestration error by step stem:[L] is

[stem]
++++
H_{MCP}(i; L)
= 1 - \mathbb{E}\Big[ \prod_{t=1}^{L-1} (1 - \varepsilon_t(h_t)) \Big]
++++

This refinement makes two points precise.

First, skinny versus wide regions now simply appear as variation in stem:[\varepsilon_t(h_t)]. Moving through a narrow, heavily constrained subgraph keeps stem:[\varepsilon_t(h_t)] close to zero; moving through a broad, weakly constrained subgraph pushes stem:[\varepsilon_t(h_t)] higher. The combinatorial picture is no longer tied to a constant branching factor stem:[d]; it is captured by the sequence of local error rates along the actual trajectory.

Second, as long as there exists any reachable prefix stem:[h_t \in R_t] with stem:[\varepsilon_t(h_t) > 0], the overall hallucination probability stem:[H_{MCP}(i; L)] is strictly greater than zero for sufficiently long workflows. In other words, local structure can reduce risk in specific regions of the graph, but unless every reachable decision point has zero local error, the global orchestration error probability cannot be eliminated.

A standard inequality further clarifies how cumulative risk grows. For any non-negative sequence stem:[\{\varepsilon_t\}],

[stem]
++++
\prod_{t=1}^{L-1} (1 - \varepsilon_t)
\le e^{-\sum_{t=1}^{L-1} \varepsilon_t} 
++++

This bounds the all-steps-valid probability from above. Equivalently, it bounds the orchestration error probability from below:

[stem]
++++
H_{MCP}(i;L) \ge 1 - e^{-\sum_{t=1}^{L-1} \varepsilon_t} .
++++

Here stem:[\sum_t \varepsilon_t] is the cumulative local hazard along the workflow: a hazard-style quantity obtained by summing the stepwise local error rates, not itself a probability and therefore not bounded by stem:[1]. For example, eight steps each contributing a local error rate of stem:[0.125] give stem:[\sum_t \varepsilon_t = 1]. In that case the probability of remaining valid is at most stem:[e^{-1} \approx 0.368], so the probability of at least one orchestration error is at least stem:[1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632], or about 63.2%. If stem:[\sum_t \varepsilon_t = 2], then the all-steps-valid probability is at most stem:[e^{-2} \approx 0.135], and the error probability is at least stem:[1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.865], or about 86.5%.

Applied to stem:[\varepsilon_t(h_t)], this shows that if the expected cumulative hazard stem:[\sum_t \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t(h_t)]] grows with stem:[L], then the probability that all steps remain valid decays exponentially, and the probability of at least one orchestration error tends toward one in the limit.

=== Safety Interlocks and Ever-Error Probability

Another natural counterargument is that real systems often deploy safety checks, validators, and hard stops, so invalid orchestration attempts may be caught before they can do harm. This objection is important, and it is not merely hypothetical: current practice includes layered prompt-injection defenses, output validators, runtime guardrails around tool calls, and structured prompt or tool interfaces pass:[\cite{liu2024promptinjectiondefenses,alzahrani2026promptguard,openai2026guardrails,chen2025struq,wallace2024instructionhierarchy}]. But the objection must be parsed carefully. A guard that blocks an invalid action after the policy has already selected it is not the same thing as a mechanism that removes that action from the policy's reachable choice set in the first place. The real question is therefore not only whether invalid workflows can sometimes be intercepted after selection, but how far the invalid region of action space is actually collapsed before the probabilistic policy acts.

This distinction separates two regimes. In the first, an invalid action remains available to the policy but is later caught by an interlock; this can reduce downstream harm without eliminating the non-zero probability of attempting the bad move. Output guardrails, critic-style validators, and some runtime checks around tool execution often operate in this fashion pass:[\cite{alzahrani2026promptguard,openai2026guardrails}]. In the second, invalid actions are removed upstream by deterministic restriction of the admissible next-step set. Structured queries, role separation, and instruction-priority mechanisms aim at this stronger form of control by shrinking the reachable invalid space before generation is allowed to act on it pass:[\cite{chen2025struq,wallace2024instructionhierarchy,alzahrani2026promptguard}]. That second regime genuinely shrinks the reachable invalid space, and in the limit it ceases to be open-ended agentic orchestration. While the literature is already moving in this direction, we argue here that the underlying move can be formalized directly as a collapse of invalid action space. The present subsection addresses the first regime directly and shows why post-selection blocking alone does not remove ever-error probability.

To represent this cleanly, extend the graph stem:[G] with two absorbing states:

- stem:[v_{ok}], representing successful completion of a clinically valid workflow.
- stem:[v_{fail}], representing an invalid or blocked workflow, including cases where safety interlocks halt execution.

The orchestration process under intent stem:[i] can then be seen as a Markov chain on this extended graph. For a given policy stem:[\pi_{MCP}], define the ever-error probability

[stem]
++++
H^{ever}_{MCP}(i)
= \mathbb{P}_{\pi_{MCP}(\cdot \mid i)}\big[ \text{the induced path hits } v_{fail} \text{ at least once} \big] .
++++

Safety interlocks in this first, post-selection sense change what happens after stem:[v_{fail}] is reached, but they do not change the probability that the policy attempts to move into the invalid region. Formally, suppose there exists a reachable state stem:[x] and action stem:[a] such that

[stem]
++++
\mathbb{P}[x \to v_{fail} \mid a] > 0
\quad \text{and} \quad
\pi_{MCP}(a \mid x) > 0 .
++++

Then the ever-error probability satisfies

[stem]
++++
H^{ever}_{MCP}(i)
\ge \mathbb{P}[\text{reach } x] \cdot \pi_{MCP}(a \mid x) \cdot \mathbb{P}[x \to v_{fail} \mid a]
> 0 .
++++

This inequality captures a simple but important point. Even with strong safety interlocks, as long as there is any reachable decision point where the policy assigns non-zero probability to an action that leads into stem:[v_{fail}], the probability that the system ever attempts an invalid orchestration remains strictly positive. Guards can prevent some bad workflows from completing, but they do not convert a probabilistic policy into a deterministic one.

The stronger the upstream restriction becomes, the more the system moves away from open-ended agentic orchestration and toward deterministic intent-conditioned execution. In that sense, truly effective safety does not merely catch bad choices after the fact; it progressively collapses the invalid region of action space itself. The next step is to formalize that collapse as an explicit architectural principle rather than leaving it implicit in ad hoc guardrails.

=== IAS as a Collapsing Map on Workflow Space

The Intent–Action Service (IAS) changes the problem fundamentally. Instead of allowing the agent to select paths in stem:[\mathcal{S}(G)] directly, IAS provides a deterministic compilation from intents to workflows. Formally, we define stem:[F : I \to \mathcal{S}_{valid}], where stem:[I] is the set of admissible intents and

[stem]
++++
F(i) = s_i ,
++++

with stem:[s_i \in \mathcal{S}_{valid}] for each intent stem:[i]. The mapping stem:[F] is implemented by domain experts and verified separately from any generative model.

Under IAS, the action space is reduced to intent specification followed by IAS invocation. The effective policy over workflows becomes

[stem]
++++
\pi_{IAS}(s \mid i) =
\begin{cases}
1, & s = s_i, \\
0, & s \neq s_i,
\end{cases}
++++

and the orchestration hallucination probability is

[stem]
++++
H_{IAS}(i)
= \mathbb{P}_{s \sim \pi_{IAS}(\cdot \mid i)}[s \in \mathcal{S}_{invalid}]
= 0 .
++++

The impact on the graph space is stark. In the original setting, the agent navigates a space of size roughly stem:[d^L] per intent. Under IAS, each intent corresponds to a single canonical workflow stem:[s_i]. The clinically dangerous space of invalid workflows is never touched at runtime; valid workflows are selected by construction.

One may view stem:[F] as inducing an equivalence relation on stem:[\mathcal{S}_{valid}], where all clinically acceptable workflows that serve intent stem:[i] are identified with the canonical representative stem:[s_i]. The agent is allowed to operate only on these canonical elements; it is no longer permitted to explore the full combinatorial space.

=== Residual Locations of Hallucination

This framework does not claim that hallucination disappears entirely. It clarifies where it can still reside.

The first locus of uncertainty is the mapping from natural-language user input stem:[u] to the formal intent stem:[i]. The generative model defines a distribution stem:[\rho(i \mid u)] over intents. Misinterpretation of stem:[u] leads to the selection of a wrong intent, which is a semantic error rather than an orchestration error. IAS does not, by itself, eliminate this risk; it ensures that once an intent has been selected, the corresponding workflow is correctly constructed.

The second locus arises within IAS itself. If stem:[F] is incorrectly defined so that some stem:[s_i \notin \mathcal{S}_{valid}], then the error lies in the deterministic compiler, not in the generative agent. At that point, the problem is one of software verification, governance, and clinical validation. It is no longer a hallucination in the probabilistic sense; it is a mis-specification in a conventional system.

Thus, in the MCP+agent case, hallucinations live in stem:[\pi_{MCP}(\cdot \mid i)] over the path space stem:[\mathcal{S}(G)]. In the IAS case, hallucinations are structurally removed from stem:[\mathcal{S}(G)] and confined to the mapping from user text stem:[u] to intent stem:[i], and to the correctness of stem:[F]. The enormous invalid region stem:[\mathcal{S}_{invalid}] is no longer directly exposed.

This theoretical structure provides a foundation for the central claim of this paper: the size of the dangerous space under MCP-style orchestration grows exponentially with workflow length, while IAS collapses that space to a singleton per intent, driving orchestration hallucination probability to zero by construction and localizing residual uncertainty to interpretable boundaries.

As a concise way to summarize the inevitability result, consider the set stem:[R] of states reachable under intent stem:[i]. If there exists at least one reachable state stem:[x \in R] and action stem:[a] such that the resulting workflow is clinically invalid (or leads to stem:[v_{fail}]) and stem:[\pi_{MCP}(a \mid x) > 0], then the orchestration hallucination probability satisfies

[stem]
++++
H_{MCP}(i) > 0 .
++++

If, in addition, the system is used repeatedly or workflows can grow in length so that such risky decision points are revisited, then under mild recurrence conditions the probability of at least one orchestration error approaches one as the number of decisions grows. The only way to make stem:[H_{MCP}(i)] exactly zero for all intents is to restrict the policy support so that it never selects any action that leads to an invalid workflow at any reachable state. That restriction is exactly what the IAS mapping stem:[F] enforces: it collapses the agent’s control over stem:[\mathcal{S}(G)] to a single, prevalidated choice per intent.

== Agentic Reasoning and the Limits of “Showing the Steps”

With this formalization in hand, it becomes easier to articulate why simply forcing explicit intermediate reasoning traces does not solve the problem of hallucination. The framework above was introduced in terms of tool and workflow selection, but it extends naturally to any agentic reasoning process whose intermediate outputs carry procedural force. When an LLM system emits chains of thought, code, or tool invocations, it is still sampling from a distribution over sequences. Each additional line of reasoning or program fragment is another term in a sequence akin to stem:[s = (v_1, \dots, v_L)], only now the “tools” are abstract operations in a symbolic reasoning trace. Once those intermediate outputs are allowed to determine which branch to take, which code to run, which tool to call, or which state to update, they have become part of the same induced path through executable action space.

Even when the emitted code is syntactically valid and apparently deterministic, its semantics may be incorrect. It may invoke the wrong tools, apply the wrong thresholds, or depend on incorrectly retrieved or incorrectly parsed evidence. None of this is prevented by transparency. If the model’s own intermediate outputs are then treated as the basis for trusting its later outputs, one has built an echo chamber rather than a grounding mechanism.

The theoretical picture remains the same: the agent’s policy stem:[\pi] has non-zero mass on invalid elements, and the space of elements increases with the length of the reasoning chain. Asking the model to be more explicit does not change the underlying combinatorics; it simply exposes more of the generative process. By contrast, intermediate states generated or validated by external deterministic operations can meaningfully constrain later choices, because they do not merely repeat the model’s own probabilistic claims back to itself.

In safety-critical domains such as clinical imaging, this is not sufficient. A human reviewer cannot be expected to audit every detail of every reasoning chain, and in practice the very volume of reasoning produced by a capable agent can have a numbing effect, leading to over-trust rather than vigilance.

== IAS and LLMs as Points on a Spectrum

The contrast between MCP+agent and IAS is not purely dichotomous. It is helpful to think of a spectrum along which systems can be placed. At one end, there is a fully agentic configuration in which the LLM determines the selection and ordering of primitives, with minimal structural constraints. At the other, there is a configuration in which the LLM maps inputs to a discrete set of high-level intents, each deterministically compiled into a workflow.

Most real systems may fall somewhere between these extremes. They may expose a small number of macro-tools in addition to primitives, or allow a limited set of decisions within a largely deterministic pipeline. A particularly important intermediate case is a state-grounded constrained agent: the system materializes workflow state outside the model, recomputes the current admissible next actions from that state, and allows the LLM to operate only within that reduced local action set. In graph terms, the model no longer samples over the full reachable path space, but over a state-conditioned admissible region whose branching factor is smaller than that of the unconstrained graph. This can lower local error rates substantially, especially when workflow position is reconstructed from external evidence rather than conversational memory, but it does not drive them to zero unless the remaining mapping from intent to execution is deterministic. The key observation from the theoretical model is that the more of the action space is pre-collapsed into deterministic mappings like stem:[F], the smaller the region in which hallucinations can operate.

This staged form can be written compactly. In the pure one-shot IAS case, a user expression stem:[u] is interpreted as an intent and deterministically compiled into a full workflow:

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
u &\xrightarrow{\rho} i \\
  &\xrightarrow{F} s_i
\end{aligned}
++++

In open agentic orchestration, by contrast, the model induces an action sequence directly:

[stem]
++++
\begin{aligned}
u &\xrightarrow{\pi} a_1 \\
  &\xrightarrow{\pi} a_2 \\
  &\xrightarrow{\pi} \cdots \\
  &\xrightarrow{\pi} a_L
\end{aligned}
++++

For complex workflows, one can apply the IAS principle repeatedly at each externally materialized state. The key object is now the state node itself. At each state, the admissible local choices are determined externally, and IAS resolves the next action from that state rather than asking the model to author the whole downstream procedure in one shot. The local transition rule is simply

[stem]
++++
x_t \xrightarrow{\,F_{x_t}(i)\,} a_t \xrightarrow{\,M\,} x_{t+1}.
++++

Here stem:[x_t] denotes an externally materialized system state, stem:[i] a locally admissible intent available at that state, stem:[a_t] the executable action or subworkflow selected by IAS for that intent, stem:[F_{x_t}] the deterministic state-conditioned intent-to-action map, and stem:[M] the external deterministic materialization/update process that writes the next state. Branching still exists, but it exists between externally grounded states rather than inside a free-running agent policy. At each node stem:[x_t], IAS collapses the local choice to one valid outgoing action for the selected intent; after execution, stem:[M] materializes the next state, and only then is the next local choice exposed.

For intuition, one may picture a staged IAS workflow as a branching tree of externally materialized states:

[source,text]
----
                           x0
                         ╱    ╲
          F_x0(i_a) -> a_a    a_b <- F_x0(i_b)
                       │        │
                       M        M
                       │        │
                      x1a      x1b
                    ╱   ╲     ╱   ╲
   F_x1a(i_c) -> a_c   a_d   a_e   a_f <- F_x1b(i_f)
                  │     │     │     │
                  M     M     M     M
                  │     │     │     │
                 x2a   x2b   x2c   x2d
----

The critical point is that the state that counts as operational reality is not authored by the agent's own narration. The nodes stem:[x_t] are produced by deterministic external operations that the agent may invoke, but does not define. Pure IAS collapses procedural freedom in one hop; staged IAS applies the same collapse repeatedly at each externally grounded branching point.

For clinical applications, the argument made here is that systems must be designed as close as possible to the IAS end of the spectrum. Generative models are used for interpretation and interaction, but not for determining the procedural structure of workflows.

== Experimental Evaluation: A Narrative Outline

The theoretical arguments suggest that agentic orchestration and IAS-based orchestration will behave very differently in practice, particularly in terms of reproducibility and safety. These differences can be tested in a controlled experimental setting.

One can construct a testbed based on an imaging platform where the underlying tools and workflows are well understood. In the agentic condition, the LLM is given access to primitive imaging operations through a tool registry, and workflow specifications corresponding to clinical intents are generated, for example for lesion segmentation, quality control, and result export. The system logs the sequence of tool calls, their parameters, and any errors encountered. By repeating the same intent multiple times, one can measure how frequently the system yields different workflows for the same request, how often required steps are omitted, and how often invalid combinations of tools are attempted.

In the IAS condition, the same LLM receives only one available action at the tool level: the ability to invoke the IAS with a specified intent. The IAS then constructs and executes the workflow deterministically. Repeated trials under this condition should produce identical workflows and, assuming correct IAS specification, no invalid sequences. The only variability should appear in how the LLM renders the intent or maps an input into a small set of available intents, not in the structure of the workflows themselves.

A secondary dimension of the experiment can involve human evaluation. Clinicians or domain experts can be presented with the workflows produced under each condition, without knowing how they were generated, and asked to assess their plausibility, completeness, and safety. If the theory is correct, IAS-generated workflows should be more consistent, more auditable, and easier to trust.

== Discussion

The formulation presented here reframes hallucination not as a purely epistemic failure of generative models, but as a structural property of systems that require these models to navigate large action spaces. As long as a generative agent is responsible for selecting or constructing workflows from primitives, it will exhibit non-zero orchestration hallucination probability. Adding more tools and more reasoning steps only expands and deepens the region in which this probability is realized.

The Intent–Action Service pattern offers a complementary perspective. It acknowledges that generative models are powerful at interpreting language and mapping informal descriptions to formal categories, but insists that the translation from these categories to executable workflows be handled by a deterministic, separately validated system. In doing so, IAS collapses the action graph, removing the agent from the most dangerous part of the decision chain.

It is important to be explicit about the limitations of this approach. IAS does not guarantee correctness of the workflows themselves; that remains the responsibility of clinical governance and software verification. Nor does it prevent misinterpretation of user intents. However, it draws a clear boundary: if an error occurs in execution, it is attributable to a deterministic component. This makes the system amenable to traditional forms of testing, certification, and regulation.

This suggests an MCP–IAS spectrum. At the fully agentic end, LLM outputs determine the discovery and ordering of primitive tools exposed via MCP, with maximal flexibility and maximal action-space risk. In the middle, MCP exposes coarser “macro-tools” that encapsulate validated sub-workflows, reducing branching but leaving some orchestration to the model. At the deterministic end, the LLM maps inputs to intents and passes them to IAS, which compiles to a single canonical workflow per intent. Movement along this spectrum trades runtime flexibility for reproducibility and auditability; the argument here is that safety-critical clinical systems should reside near the IAS end. Formal verification work (e.g., Reluplex, AI2 abstract interpretation) shows how hard it is to certify arbitrary neural policies at scale \cite{katz2017reluplex,gehr2018ai2}; collapsing orchestration into deterministic pipelines is a more tractable safety surface.

Retrieval-augmented systems such as Openevidence sit in this middle region. By conditioning generation on cited literature, they shrink the semantic space and improve auditability, but they do not eliminate probabilistic failure modes \cite{xu2024impossible,smith2025agentic}. Citation mismatch (a true citation that does not support the generated claim) and unsupported synthesis remain common RAG errors \cite{smith2025agentic,liu2024faithfulness}. In orchestration terms, retrieval constrains content selection but leaves the sequence of tool calls (retrieve, filter, rank, summarize) under a probabilistic policy. IAS addresses a different layer: once an intent is selected, the workflow is deterministic. Openevidence-style RAG can complement IAS for evidence retrieval, but it does not obviate the need to collapse procedural action graphs.

Code-generating RAG systems, where the model emits Python to process retrieved data and plot results, reduce some failure modes but do not remove the probabilistic boundary. Retrieval can still fetch misaligned or incomplete evidence; code synthesis can introduce incorrect filters, statistical tests, or visualizations; and small prompt variations can yield different pipelines for the same question \cite{chen2023pal,wang2024codepolicies}. Graphical outputs can make incorrect computations look plausible unless every generated script is independently verified. Fixed, prevalidated pipelines (as in IAS) collapse this procedural space; ad hoc generated programs do not.

There are deeper limits on “making agents deterministic.” NLG and generative surveys document persistent hallucination and faithfulness gaps even under heavy constraints \cite{ji2023survey,maynez2020faithfulness,liu2024faithfulness,bender2021parrots}. Underspecification work shows models with identical training loss can behave divergently under shift, undermining reproducibility \cite{damour2020underspec,recht2019imagenet}. Attempts to verify arbitrary neural programs face undecidability and intractability barriers; practical verifiers only handle narrow cases \cite{katz2017reluplex,gehr2018ai2}. Chasing determinism in the generative policy collapses, in the limit, to a lookup table that cannot generalize, an uninteresting edge case that abandons the benefits of generative modeling. Determinism must instead be imposed on the orchestration layer, not the generative core.

Guardrails and self-checks have not closed this gap. Empirical studies show that detector-based or self-consistency approaches (e.g., SelfCheckGPT) reduce but do not eliminate hallucinations, often missing fluent but unsupported claims \cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt}. Factuality benchmarks (TruthfulQA, factual summarization) remain unsolved despite prompt tuning and guardrail policies \cite{lin2022truthfulqa,pagnoni2021factuality}. Safety frameworks (e.g., NeMo/Guardrails-style filters) mitigate some failure modes but do not change the underlying probability mass over invalid generations; hallucination remains a structural property of the agentic policy \cite{openai2024mitigating}. In short, agent-space guardrails trim the tails but cannot make the distribution deterministic.

Recent agent surveys complement this argument with a state-space/process lens. Lin et al. model agents as POMDPs and show hallucinations can arise across perception, planning, and action \cite{lin2025agentsurvey}. Our formulation uses an action-graph/combinatorial lens: \(|\mathcal{S}(G)|\) grows rapidly with tools and steps, and any non-zero mass on invalid paths yields irreducible orchestration risk. The two views converge: whether framed as stochastic transitions in a POMDP or as a probability distribution over action paths, agentic policies retain unavoidable failure probability; removing it requires collapsing the action space into deterministic, prevalidated workflows (IAS).

== Conclusion

The enthusiasm surrounding agentic AI in clinical settings often overlooks a simple mathematical fact: as the space of possible action sequences grows, the probability of error for a probabilistic agent that must navigate that space cannot be driven to zero. Requiring agents to expose their reasoning does not alter this; it increases the number of steps and therefore the opportunities for deviation.

By introducing an Intent–Action Service that maps high-level intents to canonical workflows, it is possible to restructure the problem. Instead of asking generative models to construct procedures, we ask them only to select or formulate intents. Execution is then delegated to a deterministic compiler that operates in a much smaller, carefully curated space. The result is not the elimination of hallucination, but its containment.

For clinical workflows, where deterministic behavior and auditability are non-negotiable, this architectural separation between generative interpretation and deterministic execution may be the only viable way to harness the flexibility of generative models without inheriting their combinatorial risks.
